# Such a Thing as Society

*On the cost of neglect as economic policy*

---

In 2021, the United States expanded the Child Tax Credit — a monthly payment to families with children, no strings attached. The Supplemental Poverty Measure for children dropped to 5.2%, the lowest rate ever recorded. In 2022, Congress let the expansion expire. Child poverty doubled to 12.4% in a single year — the largest increase in over fifty years of measurement.¹ Roughly 5.2 million children crossed the poverty line in one direction, then crossed it back in the other, in the time it took a provision to lapse. Not a recession. Not a policy failure in the traditional sense. A subtraction. One dimension removed; the product collapsed.

That word — *product* — is precise, and it matters.

• • •

A mathematical result I've been working on — proven axiomatically, validated across eight empirical domains — shows that when independent dimensions combine to produce an emergent outcome, the composition is multiplicative, not additive.² The emergent property is the *product* of its dimensions, not their sum. The proof follows from five constraints that are difficult to argue with: if any dimension is absent, the outcome is zero; the function is continuous and monotonic; it's scale-consistent; and the dimensions don't interact through hidden terms. Given these, the multiplicative form is the only valid composition function. The math doesn't offer an alternative.

The consequence that matters for policy is immediate. If the composition is multiplicative, then the partial derivative — the marginal return on investment in any single dimension — scales inversely with that dimension's current value. The lower the dimension, the higher the return. Not as a moral claim. As calculus. ∂E/∂xᵢ is proportional to 1/xᵢ. When xᵢ is near zero, the derivative is enormous. When xᵢ is already large, the derivative is negligible.

The returns are at the bottom.

And if any dimension reaches zero, the product is zero — regardless of how large every other dimension is. That's zero-collapse. No compensation possible. The system doesn't degrade proportionally; it falls off a cliff.

• • •

Consider a person without stable housing. If the ability to function composes multiplicatively from housing stability × healthcare access × education × financial security × social connection, then a person with zero housing gets zero return from job training. Not reduced returns. Zero. The product collapses regardless of how much you invest in the other dimensions. The job training doesn't land because the person can't sleep, can't shower, can't keep a schedule, can't store documents. Every other investment multiplies by zero.

This isn't theory. It's what Housing First programs discovered empirically — and the data is unambiguous. The CDC's Community Preventive Services Task Force reviewed 26 studies and found that Housing First programs decrease homelessness, increase stability, and improve quality of life; every dollar invested returns $1.44 in savings elsewhere.³ The Canadian At Home/Chez Soi trial — a randomized controlled study across five cities, 950 participants — found that Housing First achieved 73% stable housing at one year versus 31% for treatment-as-usual; 69% of the intervention's cost was offset by reduced use of emergency services, shelters, and hospitals.⁴ Finland adopted Housing First as national strategy in 2008. Long-term homelessness fell 68% over fourteen years. Finland is the only country in the European Union where homelessness has declined.⁵

The conventional approach — job training here, a clinic visit there, a food bank on Thursdays — is implicitly additive. It assumes each intervention adds value independently. The multiplicative result says that's structurally wrong. If housing is zero, the food bank adds to zero. If healthcare access is zero, the job training multiplies by zero. You're investing in dimensions that can't produce returns because the product is collapsed by the dimension you're not addressing.

Housing First works not because housing is more important than healthcare or education. It works because housing was the zero — and until you address the zero, nothing else multiplies. The piecemeal approach keeps investing in nonzero dimensions while the zero dimension swallows the product.

The "bootstrap" narrative — work harder, invest in yourself, pull yourself up — is mathematically incoherent when any dimension is at zero. No amount of effort in the nonzero dimensions compensates. The math doesn't have a bootstrap exception.

• • •

Scale up. If a country's economic resilience composes multiplicatively from its dimensions — safety nets, healthcare access, employment, infrastructure, institutional trust — then during a crisis, the dimensions nearest zero produce the highest marginal returns per dollar invested. The mathematically optimal crisis response is to invest most heavily in the lowest dimensions. Not because it's compassionate. Because it's where the derivative is steepest.

Austerity does the opposite. It cuts the dimensions closest to zero — social programs, unemployment support, healthcare access — while directing resources to dimensions already above zero: bank recapitalization, corporate tax relief, bond market confidence. The multiplicative result says this is investing where marginal returns are lowest and divesting from where they're highest.

Greece and Iceland provide the natural experiment. Both economies collapsed in 2008. Greece chose austerity: GDP fell 26% in five years; unemployment peaked at 28%; youth unemployment hit 58%; the economy shrank by a quarter. The debt-to-GDP ratio — the thing austerity was supposed to fix — climbed from 130% to 180%. Austerity didn't even achieve its own objective.⁶ Iceland chose the inverse: let the banks fail, protect domestic deposits, prosecute bankers (29 received prison sentences), invest in the safety net while the currency devalued. GDP returned to pre-crisis levels by 2015. Unemployment fell to 3%. IMF loans were repaid ahead of schedule.⁷

The United States and the United Kingdom ran the same experiment a year later. The US passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act — $836 billion in stimulus targeting unemployment compensation, state fiscal aid, infrastructure, SNAP. The CBO estimated ARRA raised real GDP by 0.7–4.1% and added up to 2.8 million jobs at peak effect.⁸ The UK chose austerity under Cameron and Osborne: capital spending fell 32% in real terms; food bank centres rose from 35 to nearly 1,300; emergency food parcels went from 60,000 to 2.89 million; homelessness rose 34%; life expectancy flatlined; and after fourteen years of cutting, government debt rose from 65% to 98% of GDP.⁹

The IMF itself — not a radical institution — admitted the error. Blanchard and Leigh found the fiscal multiplier was 0.9 to 1.7, not the 0.5 that models had assumed. Cutting the lowest dimensions cost roughly twice what the forecasters predicted.¹⁰

The Keynesian result — stimulus works better than austerity during downturns — falls out of the multiplicative math without needing macroeconomic theory. Stimulus targets the lowest dimensions. Austerity cuts them. The derivative is largest at the bottom. That's all there is to it.

• • •

Scale up further. If a society's total output composes multiplicatively from institutional trust × human capital × infrastructure × financial stability, then one dimension reaching zero doesn't produce proportional decline. It produces collapse. The cliff, not the slope.

Venezuela sits on 303 billion barrels of proven oil reserves — roughly 18% of the world total, more than Saudi Arabia.¹¹ It has labor, infrastructure, arable land. GDP peaked at $372.6 billion in 2012. By 2020, it had fallen 88% — deeper than the US Great Depression, exceeding Syria's wartime collapse. Per capita income dropped from $12,607 to $1,509. The resource endowment never changed. What changed was institutional trust: the World Bank's Rule of Law estimate fell from −0.76 to −2.35; Voice and Accountability from −0.13 (near the world average) to −1.82; Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index fell to 10 out of 100 — among the three most corrupt countries on earth.¹² One dimension went to zero. The product followed.

Zimbabwe had productive farmland and an educated workforce. After the land-reform seizures beginning in 2000, food production fell 60% in a decade. Hyperinflation peaked at 79.6 billion percent per month — prices doubling every twenty-four hours. Per capita income fell from $1,640 to $661. By 2008, GDP per capita had collapsed to a level last seen in 1952. The World Bank's Rule of Law indicator hit the 1st percentile.¹³

In both cases, large dimensions survived — oil, farmland, human capital, infrastructure. One dimension collapsed. An additive model predicts that losing one of four dimensions should reduce output by roughly a quarter. Actual decline: 78–90%. That's the cliff signature. That's the product collapsing because a single factor reached zero, exactly as the multiplicative form predicts. The zero swallowed everything.

• • •

Here's where the conventional wisdom inverts.

For forty years, the dominant economic policy framework in the United States and much of the West has been: invest at the top. Cut taxes for the wealthy and corporations; the benefits trickle down. The implicit model is additive — boost the top, the gains distribute, everyone's sum increases.

The multiplicative result says this is investing where the partial derivative is shallowest. When xᵢ is already large, ∂E/∂xᵢ is small. The returns at the top are, within measurement error, zero.

And the empirical record confirms it. Hope and Limberg examined fifty years of major tax cuts for the rich across eighteen OECD countries (1965–2015) and found no statistically significant effect on real GDP per capita or unemployment — only higher income concentration at the top.¹⁴ The Congressional Research Service found no conclusive evidence that sixty-five years of top-rate reductions were associated with saving, investment, or productivity growth — but the cuts tracked perfectly with the top 0.1% income share rising from 4.2% to 12.3%.¹⁵ The IMF concluded that the equity-efficiency trade-off is "largely illusory" — lower net inequality is robustly correlated with faster, more durable growth.¹⁶ Kansas ran what its own governor called a "real live experiment": cut the top rate 30%, tax pass-through income at zero. GDP grew at half the national rate. A Republican legislature repealed the cuts over the governor's veto.¹⁷

Now compare that to investment at the bottom. In rural Kenya, unconditional cash transfers of about $1,000 to over 10,500 poor households produced a local fiscal multiplier of 2.5 — each dollar injected at the bottom generated $2.50 in local economic activity, with large positive spillovers to non-recipients and minimal inflation.¹⁸ The BRAC graduation program — a simultaneous "big push" across multiple dimensions (asset transfer, consumption support, training, coaching, savings, health) — tested across six countries in a randomized trial produced returns of 133% to 433%, with the multidimensional package consistently outperforming single-component interventions in the same study.¹⁹ Chetty, Hendren, and Katz found that children who moved to lower-poverty neighborhoods before age thirteen earned 31% more as adults, with returns largest for those starting from the lowest baseline.²⁰

Place these side by side. Investment at the top: zero measurable growth across fifty years and eighteen countries. Investment at the bottom: multipliers of 2.5x to 4.3x in randomized trials. The juxtaposition doesn't require editorializing. Trickle-down invests where the derivative is approximately zero. Social investment invests where it's maximal. One is provably suboptimal. The other is provably optimal. The math closed the question. What remains open is why we keep choosing the wrong answer.

• • •

On July 4, 2025, the United States signed into law the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The law cuts roughly $1 trillion from Medicaid and $186 billion from SNAP over ten years — the two largest safety net programs in the country, serving the lowest dimensions of the most vulnerable populations. The same law makes permanent the 2017 tax cuts, raises the estate tax exemption to $15 million, and restores full bonus depreciation for corporations. The Congressional Budget Office's distributional analysis is unambiguous: the bottom income decile loses 3.1% of its resources; the top decile gains 2.7%.²¹

This is the multiplicative model's prediction enacted as policy: cut the lowest dimensions, subsidize the highest. The floor shrinks. The ceiling rises. The gap between them is not a side effect. It is the mechanism.

The early cliff indicators are already visible. Homelessness hit a record 771,480 in HUD's 2024 count — an 18% increase, the largest since data collection began in 2007.²² The first-ever lapse in SNAP funding, during the government shutdown of November 2025, left 42 million recipients without benefits. Food banks reported record demand.²³ The first rural hospital to attribute its closure directly to anticipated Medicaid cuts — Curtis Medical Center in Nebraska — has already shut its doors, with over 300 more rural hospitals identified as at-risk.²⁴

Meanwhile, the institutional trust dimension — the one that, in Venezuela and Zimbabwe, swallowed everything — is drifting downward. The V-Dem Institute downgraded the United States from "liberal democracy" to "electoral democracy" for the first time in over fifty years.²⁵ Freedom House gave it the lowest score since the current system began.²⁶ The Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index recorded its lowest US score since the index was created in 2006.²⁷ Gallup finds trust in the federal government mired near five-decade lows.²⁸

None of these individually constitutes collapse. But the multiplicative model doesn't require every dimension to collapse. It requires one.

• • •

The math doesn't have a political opinion. It has a structure. The structure says the returns are at the bottom — not because the bottom deserves them, not because compassion demands it, but because that's where the curve is steepest. Helping the most affected isn't charity. It's optimization. The compassionate allocation and the efficient allocation are the same allocation. They were never in tension. We just had the wrong model.

For fifty years, the dominant model has been additive: invest at the top, the gains distribute. The multiplicative result says that model is structurally incorrect, and the empirical record — across eighteen countries, six randomized trials, two failed states, and two natural experiments in crisis response — says the same thing the math says. The returns at the top are, within measurement error, zero. The returns at the bottom are 2.5 to 4.3 times the investment. The question of where to invest was never a values question. It was a math question. And it's been answered.

Whether anyone acts on the answer is a different kind of question — one the math can't touch. A system that profits from investing in the wrong place has structural reasons to keep doing so, and structural mechanisms for ensuring that the people bearing the cost blame each other rather than the allocation. That's not a mathematical observation. That's a political one. I'll leave it there.

Margaret Thatcher said there was no such thing as society. The math says there is. It says society is a product, not a sum. It says the product is only as strong as its weakest factor. And it says the returns — if anyone is interested in collecting them — are at the bottom.

They've been there the whole time.
